# Prostheti Innovations

## Prologue: The Wells Family

In the outer reaches of District 20, far enough from T Corp.'s center that the sepia drain and time distortions are bearable but close enough to inherit its culture of relentless innovation, a prosthetics company called Prostheti Innovations once operated with its doors wide open. Clyde Wells had built it from nothing -- or close to nothing. He had worked quality control at a Wing-adjacent augment manufacturer, watched them cut corners on Backstreets-grade product, filed reports that went nowhere, and eventually walked out with the blueprints for their best-selling prosthetic line. The engineering was sound; the problem had always been manufacturing shortcuts. The blueprints weren't patented Singularity tech -- just good work they'd never bothered to protect -- so no one came after him. He built the first batch by hand in a rented garage, sold them to a Fixer who liked the craftsmanship, and word of mouth did the rest. That garage became Prostheti Innovations.

Clyde and his wife Vivian ran the company together the way they believed it should be run: in person, on the ground, visible to the people they served. Vivian had earned that right. Born in the Backstreets herself, she had fought her way into the Cinq Association's South branch and climbed to Section 2 -- one tier below the Section Directors, operating at roughly Grade 3 in the broader Fixer system. In a profession where most people die young or burn out before they reach Section 4, that rank meant something. She was disciplined, precise, and known in the southern quarter for ending duels cleanly. She met Clyde through the prosthetics trade -- Fixers need augments to survive, and Prostheti Innovations was a growing name in District 20's competitive market. What started as a business relationship became a marriage, and she poured her reputation and her network into the company. A Section 2 Cinq fixer endorsing your prosthetics carries enormous weight in a world where every Fixer needs hardware to stay alive.

Under Vivian's influence, Prostheti Innovations became more than a manufacturer. She personally fitted augments for Fixers she knew, offered free maintenance to Backstreets residents who couldn't afford repairs on their cheap, glitching prosthetics, and used Cinq conferences to showcase the company's work to a wider audience. People knew Prostheti Innovations because they knew her. Clyde felt safe about this approach because his wife was a combat-tested Association professional. If trouble came to their door, she could handle it. She had handled worse.

The Insurgence Clan warned them to back off as the company grew in influence. Clyde dismissed the threat -- partly out of principle, partly because he had a Section 2 Cinq fixer at his side. The Clan did not throw a glove. They did not declare intent. They did not challenge Vivian to a duel under Cinq rules and etiquette. They exploited the Night in the Backstreets -- the 81-minute window from 3:13 to 4:34 AM when anything goes and no one is held responsible -- and struck from the shadows. An assassination during the Night is the antithesis of everything Cinq represents: no honor, no rules, no chance to respond. All her skill, her rank, her years of combat experience -- none of it mattered against an attack she never saw coming. She was killed right in front of Clyde.

Clyde withdrew entirely. The lesson he took from Vivian's death was specific and absolute: skill doesn't protect you. Visibility is what kills you. He replaced Cinq-style directness with Zwei-style protection -- hiring shields rather than trusting swords. He grew the company through patents, Inventor certifications, and economic influence from behind closed doors. He never engaged with the community again. And when his daughter Penny, now nineteen, began showing the same idealism her mother had carried -- the same conviction that showing up for people mattered more than staying safe -- Clyde saw his wife's funeral all over again.

What Clyde did not know was that the Insurgence Clan had already set its next operation in motion. Years earlier, a young man named Hector -- one of Penny's childhood friends from the old days when the company was still community-connected -- had been pulled out of Backstreets poverty by the Clan. They gave him augments, money, a place. He became an established operative, eventually running their unregistered augment shop in District 20. When the Clan's leadership identified Penny Wells as a target and discovered that one of their own people had a genuine childhood connection to her, they assigned Hector the operation. His debt left no room to refuse. But what made it bearable was that he genuinely believed he was saving her. He had seen what happened to people who tried to become Fixers in the outer Backstreets. Penny was nineteen, one year from applying for her Hana license, training in secret to throw herself into one of the most lethal professions in the City. Hector convinced himself that wherever the Clan was taking her, she would at least be alive.

The manipulation was surgical. Hector spent the first year building a disposable cover identity -- a fake job at a competitor prosthetics company, return addresses for letters -- and studying the Wells family's dynamics from a distance. Then he reconnected with Penny and began training her in combat, writing letters she treasured and he made just noticeable enough for Clyde to find. A stranger's letters might have prompted Clyde to confront Penny or investigate. But Hector's name was familiar -- a childhood friend, harmless, part of the background of their old life. The combination of a familiar name and a suspicious employer was calibrated to make Clyde start reading without escalating to action. And when Clyde read the letters and discovered that Penny was training to become a Fixer, he did not see a teenager with a hobby. He saw Vivian. The same idealism. The same willingness to be visible, to be present, to put herself in harm's way. Confronting Penny about the training meant confronting why it terrified him -- which meant talking about her mother's assassination, a conversation he had never been able to have. So he watched. Surveillance gave him the feeling of control without the risk of the conversation. For a year, he never once proved Hector wrong.

## Chapter 1: Polished Surfaces

Life at Prostheti Innovations moved to the rhythm of its factory floor. A group of new designers -- recent hires brought on to help with the company's growing augment line -- arrived and were welcomed by Clyde with professional warmth and careful distance, then set to work designing prosthetics and augments. Clyde was a good boss -- attentive to his workers, proud of the company, clearly invested in its success. But he kept everyone at arm's length, and if you watched closely, you could see the walls he had built around himself. He talked about Penny's future as though it were already decided. It was always "when she takes over," never "if she wants to."

Penny worked around the factory and made herself impossible not to like. She was action-oriented, clear-eyed about the world, and burning with a conviction that the company's strategy of growing influence from behind closed doors wasn't enough. The outer Backstreets were still full of factory workers with degrading augments they couldn't afford to fix, children carrying machine parts, and people living on the wrong side of T Corp.'s time inequality. Prostheti Innovations was doing well. The district was not. Penny didn't believe another decade of patent filings and Expo awards would change that. She wanted to become a Fixer -- not out of naivety, but because she understood the system's ugliness and believed individual choice still existed within it. An independent Office could be selective. A Fixer with genuine prosthetics knowledge could maintain their own augmentations and help injured civilians without depending on expensive Workshop contracts. She was one year from eligibility for her Hana license, and she was counting the days.

She had been training in secret with Hector, her childhood friend. Through letters -- physical mail, the only option in District 20's low-tech outer reaches -- they had reconnected, and he had been teaching her to fight. When she trusted the designers enough, she introduced them to him: a warm, encouraging presence who treated her Fixer ambitions as something worth pursuing rather than a phase to be tolerated. The designers even joined their training sessions in the neighborhoods outside the factory, and the sessions felt genuine, even joyful. Penny had a friend who showed up.

What no one could see was the architecture beneath. Clyde knew about Hector. He knew about the training. He had been intercepting Penny's letters for the entire year, reading every word, tracking her movements through details she never realized she was giving away. Small things slipped through his composure -- a comment about the weather near where Penny trained, a reference to a neighborhood he had no reason to visit, an offhand remark that showed he knew her schedule better than a distant father should. He wasn't neglecting Penny. He was watching from a distance and pretending he wasn't, because surveillance let him feel in control without the risk of speaking to her about why her Fixer dream filled him with dread. And beyond even Clyde's awareness, Hector was counting on exactly this. Every letter was a wedge. Every training session was a replacement for the relationship Clyde refused to have. Hector had become the person Clyde couldn't bring himself to be -- someone who showed up, engaged directly, and made Penny feel heard.

## Chapter 2: Paper Walls

Hector told Penny he had a test for her. If she was serious about becoming a Fixer, she needed to prove it. The job: break into a competitor's factory and destroy blueprints for a project they were developing. He insisted it had to happen that day, pushing urgency. Penny was nervous but agreed, and the designers -- who had grown close to Penny through training -- went with her. What she didn't realize -- and what anyone who understood the Fixer system would have noticed -- was that the "test" was not Fixer work at all. There was no contract, no client, no Hana-regulated job posting, and no pay. It was off-the-books corporate sabotage, the kind of work Syndicates and Rats do, not licensed professionals.

The infiltration went wrong. The blueprints were a trap. The factory director and his office workers -- augmented with their own company's best hardware -- ambushed Penny and the designers. The fight was brutal and desperate, and Penny was caught and nearly killed. Then a squad of Zwei Association Fixers broke through the walls, executed the factory director, and extracted everyone with the kind of efficient, overwhelming force that made the gap between Penny's training and real Fixer work painfully clear. This was what professional, Association-backed Fixers looked like. Penny's weekend training sessions had not prepared her for anything close to this.

Back at Prostheti Innovations, the real devastation landed. Clyde revealed that he had been intercepting and reading Penny's letters with Hector from the beginning. He had known about the secret combat training the entire time. He had allowed it to continue because he considered combat skills useful for a future CEO, and because he hoped she would eventually see the Fixer path for the dead end he believed it was. But the moment he learned she had entered the factory, he deployed the Zwei without hesitation. Hiring an Association squad cost more Ahn than most Backstreets workers see in a year. Clyde spent it like it was nothing, a quiet demonstration of the power his corporate strategy had built -- and the cage it created around Penny.

Clyde saw no issue with what he had done. He saved her life because he was paying attention, and in his mind, that validated every decision. He was not cruel. He was correct, and that was worse. But Penny's devastation went deeper than anger at being monitored. It retroactively poisoned everything. Every moment her father had been distant and uninvolved -- every time he didn't ask about her day, didn't engage with her interests, didn't talk to her about her future -- she now knew it wasn't because he didn't know. He knew everything. He had chosen to watch in silence rather than speak to her. He had a year of opportunities to have an honest conversation with his daughter, and he used every single one of them to collect information instead.

The trust shift to Hector was immediate and total. From Penny's perspective, the aftermath drew a sharp line between two men. Her father had secretly read her private letters for a year, monitored her movements, and only revealed the truth when forced to by circumstance. Hector had trained with her in person, encouraged her dreams openly, and never once violated her trust. The contrast was devastating -- and entirely by Hector's design. The "test" had never been about proving Penny could be a Fixer. Hector had alerted Clyde the moment they entered the factory, knowing Clyde would deploy the Zwei and be forced to reveal his surveillance. The entire operation -- the letters made noticeable, the year of patience, the engineered crisis -- was designed to produce exactly this result: Penny's trust in her father destroyed, her trust in Hector unshakeable. When Hector reached out to her after the fracture, offering sympathy and understanding, she had no reason to doubt him. He was the only person in her life who had been honest with her. Or so she believed.

## Chapter 3: Dead Letters

Days later, Penny was gone. She had vanished during the Night in the Backstreets, when the City's Taboo forbids recording or investigating anything that occurs during those 81 minutes. Clyde's Zwei were legally handcuffed. Hector's entire identity had dissolved overnight -- the address was empty, the competitor company had no record of him, and every return address on the old letters led nowhere. The cover identity had been designed to be disposable, and it worked perfectly.

Clyde contacted the only people he had left: the designers from his factory floor -- the same group who had trained with Penny and fought alongside her in the factory. From this point forward, they were no longer employees. They were Clyde's crew. He didn't know who had taken Penny, but he knew the threat was sophisticated -- whoever did this had access to his systems, captured her without triggering a single alarm, and avoided every blind spot in his security as if they had been studying his defenses from the inside. They had tracked his Zwei and would spot professional interference immediately. But a handful of unlicensed augment designers from his factory floor? They were nobody -- no Fixer licenses, no Association affiliation, no profiles in any system. Their anonymity was a genuine tactical advantage.

The only lead was a black truck spotted near recent disappearances in the district. At a refueling stop along an industrial stretch, the crew confronted two armed operatives posing as ordinary transport workers. One of them had been secretly messaging an unknown handler, relaying everything he learned in real time. When the confrontation turned violent, one operative -- dropping to critical condition -- detonated via a self-destruct mechanism built into his augments, killing the other in the blast. It was deliberate. Whoever was behind these people built control mechanisms directly into the hardware they gave them. The augments come with a leash.

The truck's contents told a grim story. Message logs confirmed an unknown organization already knew about Prostheti Innovations and was paranoid about someone investigating their operations -- the organization was never named in the messages. Buried in the older messages was a complaint about "the warehouse manager letting another one go" -- someone in this organization had been releasing captives instead of holding them, and the higher-ups were frustrated. And in the truck itself: records showing an augment shop operating with no TAA registration numbers, no patent filings, no safety certifications -- someone bypassing T Corp.'s entire regulatory system, selling unregistered augments that undercut legitimate manufacturers like Prostheti Innovations. Attached to those records was an address.

## Chapter 4: Old Debts

The address led the crew to a Backstreets storefront guarded by augmented operatives. Inside, a small operation: four guards, a secretary, and somewhere in the back, the boss. The guards were not ideologues. They were Backstreets people who had joined the Insurgence Clan because the City demanded you belong to something, and the Clan offered what legitimate employment wouldn't. Francis, the calm one reading a book outside, thought the boss underpriced augments and resented his terrible pay. He mentioned a warehouse run by a guy who used to be a Seven Fixer -- someone who treated his operation more like an information exchange than a holding facility and had let captives walk free more than once. Allen, the laidback one sleeping on duty, had joined because the Clan gave him hardware he couldn't afford otherwise. Martha, the idealistic one stacking cards inside, praised the Clan but had never gotten a chance to stand out. Felix, the curious one tinkering with augments, was fascinated by the technology but frustrated that the Clan wouldn't share technical details. Each of them could be swayed -- by money, by opportunity, by the promise of something the Clan wasn't providing.

The secretary arranged a meeting with the boss. The crew entered to find a figure sitting at his desk in a dark hooded cloak over glowing orange hexagonal armor plating, face hidden. The armor matched nothing known -- no manufacturer, no Workshop. It was Insurgence Clan technology, hardware of unclear origin that shouldn't exist in the Backstreets. He chatted casually, even offered items for sale. Then the act dropped.

It was Hector. Penny's childhood friend. The warm, encouraging training partner from months ago -- the presence who had made Penny feel heard. He had been running the Clan's augment shop the entire time -- the "Major Controller." A fight erupted. Guards who the crew had swayed refused to join Hector; those who hadn't fought alongside him. And when the turned guards became a problem, Hector activated a kill switch built into their augments, killing them remotely. The same pattern as the self-destructing operative on the road. The Clan's augments were never a gift. They were always a leash.

After the crew beat him down, Hector talked. The Insurgence Clan had been documenting the Wells family for years. Hector himself had joined the Clan long before the Wells operation -- pulled out of a desperate situation as a young man, he had been working within their network for years, rising through the ranks. When the Clan discovered his childhood connection to Penny, they assigned him the operation roughly two years ago. The first year was spent building his cover. The second year was the active infiltration -- the letters, the training, the manipulation of Clyde's surveillance. Every interaction with Penny had served a double purpose. The letters were designed to be found so Clyde would start tracking his own daughter -- not to feed the Clan intelligence, but to engineer the mistrust that would destroy Penny's relationship with her father. A year of secret surveillance, inevitably exposed, would shatter Penny's trust in Clyde completely and leave her with no one to turn to except Hector. And when the time came, Hector gave Penny a Nightwatcher's cloak -- gear designed for moving during the Night -- and captured her once she was outside. She was sent to an Insurgence Clan warehouse. He didn't know what happened after that.

When pressed on why -- why he would do this to someone he actually grew up with -- Hector didn't give a cold answer. He said he had seen what happens to people who try to become Fixers in the outer Backstreets. Penny was nineteen and thought weekend training made her ready. The Clan told him they wanted her alive. He convinced himself he was saving her from a worse fate. It was an uncomfortable mirror of Clyde's own logic: both men betrayed Penny's autonomy because they decided they knew better than she did about what was safe. The debt forced Hector's hand. The belief that he was protecting her let him live with it.

Clyde received Hector in custody, but the moment should not have felt triumphant, and it didn't. He had caught the man who betrayed his daughter and learned in the process that his own surveillance -- the system he had built to protect Penny -- was exactly what the Clan had wanted. The letters were made noticeable on purpose, not to feed the Clan intelligence, but to build the very betrayal that destroyed Penny's trust. Clyde's secret monitoring was the weapon Hector had built for him -- a year of hidden spying designed to shatter the relationship between father and daughter when it finally surfaced. The tool he built to protect Penny was the tool that drove her away.

## Chapter 5: Boiling Point

The warehouse from Hector's interrogation was a two-floor facility in the Backstreets, operating in Five Fingers territory with the Thumb's tacit approval. The crew's infiltration attempt failed. The Clan was prepared, and the crew were captured and restrained on the first floor.

The interrogation was conducted by Julio Smith, the warehouse's Head of Negotiation -- an ex-Seven Association Fixer whose investigative training showed in every structured question and cross-referenced detail. He was disarmingly polite for someone running a hostage operation. He offered tea -- laced with Truth Serum, though he admitted it readily enough when called out and offered to drink one himself so neither could lie. His Seven methodology was impeccable. Why he had left an Association to join the Insurgence Clan was a question that hung unanswered in the air.

Two factions occupied the warehouse, and they did not get along. Julio's Splitters wore green, carried blades, and had noticeably fewer augments -- pragmatic, less aggressive. Simon Jarvis's Steamworks goons were encased in copper and bronze steampunk armor with glowing elements and steam vents, carrying hammers, heavily augmented with Insurgence Clan hardware beneath the District 20 aesthetic. Simon himself was the Head of Security -- imposing, more machine than man, and increasingly unstable. His augments appeared to be affecting his mind.

After questioning the crew, Julio decided they were not an immediate threat and planned to move them to the second floor. Simon cracked. He attacked Julio, declaring the crew a major threat who needed to be "converted" -- not just augmented, but fundamentally changed. His language went beyond standard augmentation: stripping away what makes people human, "preparing materials" for something greater. He accused Julio of weakness, of a pattern of releasing captives, of betrayal. The argument escalated until Simon killed Julio. With his dying breath, Julio passed along the elevator code to the lower level -- one final act from a man who kept his word even when it cost him everything.

The murder triggered a full faction revolt. Steamworks goons turned on Splitters across the warehouse. In the chaos, the crew broke free and fought through steam-filled corridors and environmental hazards, descending to the lower floor. Simon's journal revealed the progression of his augment-induced corruption: growing hatred, nightmares that worsened over time, and instructions from above about "preparing materials for transport" rather than holding hostages. The language treated captives as raw resources, not people.

Simon's last stand was violent and strange. At half his strength, he glitched -- became temporarily frozen, rambling about nightmares that had grown since he started using the augments. Then he reactivated in a corrupted rage state, his systems overriding what was left of his mind. After he fell, one final exchange passed between Simon and the crew before he was gone.

Behind him were the hostages. People from multiple districts: District 20 factory workers, District 21 dockworkers grabbed near the Great Lake, District 19 immigrants recognizable by their Joseon-inspired clothing. The Clan had been kidnapping across district lines -- each district only saw a few disappearances, never the full pattern. But Penny was not among them. She had already been moved to a transport ship. She was being taken somewhere else -- out of the district, possibly out of the City entirely.

## Chapter 6: Still Water

The ship was on the Great Lake, at the border of Districts 20 and 21 -- far from U Corp.'s Portship patrols and tourist areas, out where the water gets dangerous and Whales lurk. Clyde identified a vulnerability in the hull and sent the crew in by submarine. The mission was clear: get the captain's keycard, open the captive cells, find Penny, get out.

The ship's lower deck was a gauntlet of patrolling guards and rotating cameras. The crew moved through cone-shaped sight lines, dismantled surveillance, and avoided detection as they worked their way up to the second floor. The captain's room was guarded by tougher, more alert personnel. When the captain was neutralized and his keycard taken, his computer screen flickered on.

A voice spoke. At first it sounded human -- calm, measured, addressing the crew by what they had done and how far they had come to find the girl. The tone was conversational, almost curious. But as the exchange continued, something shifted. The voice's understanding of human emotion became too precise, clinical rather than intuitive. It referred to the captives as "materials" before correcting itself. Its cadence lost the natural rhythm of human speech. It asked questions that felt more like a researcher cataloguing specimens than a person having a conversation. Then the screen distorted, and for a brief moment the crew saw it: a single glowing red eye, and behind it the sense of something vast, ancient, and utterly inhuman watching them. This was not a person. This was something beyond the City, beyond anything the Backstreets had prepared them for.

The voice remotely detonated a guard on the second floor. Blood sprayed into the lake water, and the screen went dark. A Carmine Whale surfaced from the depths, drawn by the blood -- the Clan deliberately summoning one of the Great Lake's most dangerous parasitic creatures, demonstrating knowledge of the lake's ecosystem that no City organization possessed. This was knowledge from outside the City. Dead and unconscious guards across the ship transformed into Carmine Mermaids, and the stealth mission became a frantic race against time: open cells with the keycard, escort captives to submarines, fight off the spawning horrors, escape before the ship flooded with the whale's acidic blood.

In the chaos, Penny's augments suddenly activated in overdrive. But these were not her father's augments. The Insurgence Clan had implanted their own hardware in Penny during her captivity. With the ship on the Great Lake, close enough to the Outskirts, the Tinkerer -- whatever was behind that red eye -- took direct control. Penny was not choosing to break out. She was being moved. Her body fought through anything in her way with a mechanical precision that didn't match how she fought during training. She reached a locked submarine that couldn't be opened even with the captain's keycard. The augments forced it open. She escaped alone into the lake, heading toward the Outskirts.

Penny was not rescued. Something far away pulled her out. The same pattern as the self-destructs and the kill switches: control through hardware. But this time it was being done at a distance, by something inhuman, and the person being controlled was the girl the crew had spent the entire story trying to save.

## Chapter 7: Ash and Iron

Clyde tracked Penny to the Outskirts -- the hostile wilderness beyond the City where hundreds die every week. A small human settlement had taken her in after her submarine reached shore, but they were holding her and demanding passage into the City in exchange. Clyde could not deliver this. No one could. The City's detection systems reject undocumented humans the same way they reject machines.

Clyde was done operating from behind a desk. He told the crew he was coming personally. He had built a new weapon from Hector's confiscated augments, reverse-engineered and integrated into his own hardware. He was a prosthetics manufacturer -- if anyone could jury-rig Insurgence Clan technology into something usable, it was him. But the augments weren't designed for someone outside the Clan's network. Every test fire sent pain through his body and put voices in his head he couldn't identify. He dismissed it as feedback noise. He shouldn't have.

The journey took Clyde and the crew by submarine through the Great Lake and into a landscape that was genuinely hostile in a way the City never was. No Zwei to call, no Taboo to exploit, no system to game. The crew smelled smoke before they saw the settlement. A badly injured man named Randal came running toward Clyde and the crew, covered in blood, apologizing -- sorry they held the girl hostage, sorry they made demands, begging them to understand they were desperate. Just before Clyde and the crew arrived, machines had invaded the settlement and started killing everyone. Randal thought Clyde had sent them as retaliation. When he learned Clyde hadn't, his terror deepened: if Clyde didn't send them, then who did?

The machines were unlike anything from the City. Red-eyed soldiers with overwritten wills -- captured civilians turned into obedient weapons by the Tinkerer. They moved with coordinated precision, and when they fell, the surviving machines carried away their dead rather than leave them behind. They were not mindless. Something in them still cared, even under control. Clyde fought alongside the crew, and every time he fired his weapon, he jolted in visible pain. The voices grew louder with each shot.

Between the fighting, the Outskirts showed what humanity looks like without the City's structures. Villagers turned on each other under pressure, shoving neighbors into the machines' path to buy themselves time. One man traded a life over a petty grudge -- "This is for taking my spear the other day." Others looted corpses while people were still dying. No Associations, no laws, no structure. Just people at their worst when survival was at stake.

Deeper in the burning village, Clyde spotted a young girl with augments running into a building. Every strategic instinct, every lesson from his wife's death, every wall he had built over the past eleven years -- gone in an instant. He sprinted after her, abandoning the fight mid-combat, shouting for the crew to handle the machines. The man who had spent the entire story operating from behind a desk, hiring Fixers, monitoring from a distance -- he saw his daughter and nothing else mattered.

Inside the building, a single light illuminated the far end of the room. Clyde was on the ground beneath it, his arms around Penny. He had found her. He had reached her. Then the crew saw the hole punched through his chest. Penny stood up. Clyde's body slid to the floor. Her form shifted -- limbs articulating at wrong angles, joints becoming visible, eyes going flat and dead. She was no longer Penny Wells. She was the Doll -- a humanoid machine puppet wearing Penny's face. This was what the Clan's "conversion" process produced. This was what "materials" become.

The Tinkerer descended from above and greeted the crew by name. He knew everything -- Clyde had told "Penny" the whole story before she killed him, and the Tinkerer had heard every word through her. He was not what the crew expected. He was a machine Elder -- ancient, vast in presence, speaking with a measured intelligence that made the Voice from the ship feel like an echo. His red eye was the same red eye from the computer screen. He had been watching for a long time.

He did not attack. He talked. And what he said was not the ravings of a villain.

He pointed to the villagers who had shoved each other into death over petty grudges and asked if it was surprising. Then he turned to the City itself and took it apart, piece by piece. The Night -- 81 minutes of institutionalized murder, with the Claw executing anyone who investigated and Sweepers dissolving the evidence. District 23's thriving cannibal food culture, where Syndicates kidnap civilians off streets and sell them to restaurants that serve human meat, and the Eight Chefs are respected culinary figures. The foundation of every Singularity -- every piece of technology that makes the City function -- fundamentally requiring human suffering to operate. District 20's own time inequality, where the wealthy live in faster timeframes while the poor are stuck in slow ones and children work in Backstreets factories. District 8, where H Corp. breeds humans for competition and disposes of the failures as test subjects. District 14, where one Wing sells suicide via vending machines and harvests human emotions as "Canned Experiences." District 10's gambling mines, where the indebted become slave labor. The 80,000 dead in District 22 that the City barely reacted to, because emotional numbness keeps people compliant. And the Head itself, which calls its governance "ecology" and treats humans like a garden to be pruned, ignoring inhuman experiments and mass death unless its specific Taboos are broken.

Then he told the crew what happened to his people. The Resurgence Clan -- thousands of machines who had lived in the Outskirts for millennia. The Warlord had convinced them to migrate toward the City, a journey that took decades across the desert, sustained only by the dream that the City would accept them. When they arrived, Z-Corp Security surrounded them and opened fire without warning or negotiation, with weapons that melted machines instantly. His best friend -- the Warlord, the one who believed the City would empathize -- sacrificed himself to hold open an escape route so the survivors could flee. Now they were trapped. The Great Migration was a one-way journey. There was no settlement to return to, no safe haven behind them. They were pinned against the walls of a City that would never open its gates. The remaining Elders hid the truth to preserve hope. And the Tinkerer had been exiled by his own people for trying to resurrect the Warlord -- consuming the cores of dozens of civilians, because he could not accept that the one person who believed in humanity was dead.

His conclusion was simple. If machines cannot become human -- if the City will never accept them -- then the reverse must happen. Humans must become machines. Not killed. Not conquered. Fixed. Strip away the emotions that drive cruelty, the fear that drives persecution, the tribalism that makes humans see anything different as a threat. He didn't want to destroy humanity. He wanted to cure it.

Then he turned to Penny specifically. He told the crew he understood the Wells family's dysfunction intimately. A father who watched his wife die and decided the answer was control -- surveillance, restriction, reshaping his daughter into a CEO who would carry his strategy forward. And a daughter too naive to see the danger she was walking into, too trusting to recognize a weapon disguised as a friend. Clyde saw the problem clearly: Penny was not ready for this world. The Tinkerer agreed with Clyde's diagnosis. Where they differed was the prescription. Clyde tried to fix Penny by controlling her environment. It failed. The Tinkerer's solution was more direct: fix the person. Strip away the naivety, the emotional attachments, the reckless idealism that made her exploitable. Penny had trusted Hector because she felt loyalty. She had wanted to be a Fixer because she felt compassion. She had defied her father because she felt conviction. Every emotion that made her who she was also made her a target. The Tinkerer removed those emotions. The Doll standing beside him would never be betrayed, because she would never trust. She would never be exploited, because she would never feel. In his eyes, he had finished what Clyde started. He had made Penny safe.

He offered a deal. Nanites that would transform one limb into a prosthetic and permanently lock away the memory that caused the most pain -- the fear, the irrationality, gone. He was completely transparent about what the nanites would do. He wanted trust, not deception. He would also let Penny go. But she would remain as she was: emotionless, a shell. The Penny who wanted to be a Fixer was gone. The Tinkerer considered this a kindness.

When the offer was refused, he expressed genuine disappointment. Then the Doll attacked -- devastating, precise, nothing like the Penny who had sparred with the crew months ago. She built momentum through evasion and unleashed counters with mechanical perfection. The Tinkerer coordinated with her, ancient and powerful, something from outside the City that had survived for thousands of years. When the Doll's body should have given out, she activated a last-stand protocol -- "By your Command" -- and kept fighting at the edge of destruction. The Tinkerer taunted: everything he had offered -- freedom from pain, from fear, from humanity's cruelty -- and the crew chose to suffer. This was why humanity needed to be fixed.

Then Clyde rose.

The hole was still in his chest, but he was standing. The Insurgence Clan technology he had built into his weapon -- Hector's augments, the same hardware that caused him pain and voices -- had kept his body running past the point where it should have stopped. He took aim at the Tinkerer and shouted that he would never let anyone take control of his daughter. He fired. The shot staggered the Tinkerer. The Doll collapsed, the control link severed. The Tinkerer retreated into the Outskirts, damaged, taking his surviving machines with him. He was not destroyed. He would return. But not today.

With the Tinkerer's direct link severed, the Doll form collapsed, and Penny partially regained awareness. Not fully herself, not fully the Doll. Somewhere in between. Her eyes were unfocused, her movements uncertain. She recognized the crew. She recognized her father. She didn't understand where she was or what had happened, but she was present -- fragmented, confused, but not gone. The conversion was not complete, or not irreversible. There was still something of Penny Wells in there.

Clyde was dying. The technology had held him together through force, and now that force was fading. The voices were louder than ever. He could feel the Tinkerer's pull on the augments. If the Tinkerer got within range again, Clyde would become another puppet. But for the first time in the entire story, Clyde did not make a decision for someone else. He did not tell the crew to take Penny home. He did not give orders.

He spoke to Penny. Directly. For the first time, he didn't talk about her or make decisions for her -- he talked to her. He told her he was wrong. Not about wanting to protect her -- he would never apologize for that -- but about how he did it. Reading her letters. Tracking her movements. Deciding her future without asking what she wanted. The Tinkerer said he finished what Clyde started, and Clyde couldn't argue, because the Tinkerer was right. Clyde tried to fix Penny by controlling her world. The Tinkerer fixed her by controlling her mind. The methods were different. The principle was the same: someone else deciding who Penny should be.

He told her she had the right to choose her own path. Even if it was dangerous. Even if it terrified him. Even if she chose to become a Fixer and it got her killed -- that was her choice to make, and he should have let her make it years ago. Then he asked Penny -- not the crew, Penny -- to decide what happened to her father. The Insurgence tech was going to take him. He could feel it. He was done choosing things for her.

Penny -- fragmented, barely present, struggling to process what had been done to her -- was given the one thing no one had offered her in the entire story: a choice. The crew stepped back. Clyde waited. And Penny decided. However she chose -- to honor his request, to refuse, to ask for help -- it was hers. The first real choice anyone had given her.

They left the burning village together. Penny walked out of the Outskirts -- unsteady, supported, damaged, but moving under her own power. She was not carried. She was not dragged. She chose to walk. She chose to leave. She chose.

Clyde was gone. His fate decided by the daughter he spent the entire story trying to protect and, in the end, finally trusted. The Tinkerer was still out there. The Insurgence Clan was still operating. Somewhere on the outskirts of the City's walls, the Resurgence Clan -- the peaceful machines who just wanted to be human -- were still camped, still hoping, trapped by a journey that was supposed to save them. The City didn't know what was coming. The City didn't care.

The story was not over. But for the first time, what happened next was Penny's decision.
